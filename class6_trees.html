01

Decision tree

Most of the models we have met so far are linear: they are built on a weighted sum of the features, one coefficient per feature.
Least squares, ridge, the lasso and logistic regression all work this way, and so does every polynomial or interaction term from class 2. Only KNN did not.
Some problems are not linear: an effect may appear only above a threshold, or only in one group of subjects.

Today we meet a new non-linear model: the decision tree.
A tree predicts by asking a series of yes-or-no questions about the features, such as "did this subject sleep less than 6 hours?".
Each answer sends the subject into a smaller group, and the prediction for a group is the mean y of its training subjects.

Let's build one by hand, on simulated data.
The data contains 120 subjects that completed a reaction time task, and for each of them we have two features: their age, and how hours of sleep in the night before.

We cut the age-by-sleep plane with straight lines - which correspond to splits/nodes in the tree. The result is the plane cut into rectangles/boxes, where each box is a prediction with the mean reaction time of the subjects contained in its borders.

Each dot below is one subject, colored by their reaction time (color bar on the right).

Choose a feature, then click inside the plot to split at that point. 
Try to make the RSS (sum of squared errors) as small as you can with a few cuts.
Look for blocks of subjects that share a shade, and cut along their edges.

The tree under the plot is the same model, drawn as a flowchart. Every cut we made is one split in it. That's the decision tree!
To predict a new subject, we start at the top and answer the questions, going left for "yes" and right for "no", until we reach the bottom. The mean there is the prediction.

The parts of a tree

  • Root: the first split, at the top. Every subject passes through it.
  • Internal node: any other question, below the root.
  • Branches: the two answers that leave a node, "yes" to the left and "no" to the right.
  • Leaf, or terminal node: the end of a path, where no more questions are asked. Each leaf is one box in the plot, and predicts the mean y of its training subjects.
  • Depth: the number of splits on the way from the root to a leaf.

Traditionally, our tree grows upside down: the root at the top, and the leaves at the bottom.

Key idea

A decision tree cuts the feature space into boxes, one question at a time, and predicts the mean of the training subjects in each box. The boxes and the tree are two drawings of the same model.

02

Growing a tree

Now we want an automatic process to find the cuts.
The goal is to divide the feature space into J boxes, R1, …, RJ, that make the RSS as small as possible:

RSS = JΣj=1 Σi ∈ Rj (yi − yj)2

where yj is the mean y of the training subjects in box Rj.

But, we cannot simply try every possible set of boxes.
Even with 120 subjects and two features, the first cut alone can be made in 238 ways: in any of the 119 gaps between neighbouring subjects, on either feature. Every cut after it multiplies the number of possible trees by about as many again.

Instead, trees are grown by recursive binary splitting, which is:

  • Top-down: it starts with all the subjects in one box, the root, and cuts from there.
  • Greedy: at every step it makes the cut that lowers the RSS the most right now, without looking ahead to the cuts that could follow.

At every step it tries every box, every feature Xj and every cutpoint s: the box is split into the subjects with Xj < s and the ones with Xj ≥ s, and the cut with the lowest RSS wins.
Only the gaps between neighbouring subjects need to be tried, so we have n values to test for each feature.

The first cut is on age, at 65, and on its own it does little: it lowers the RSS by only a tenth.
But it opens the way. The second cut, at age 50, lowers the RSS more than three times as much, and the third cuts at age 35.
Each of the four age bands is then cut by sleep, at its own height: about 7 hours below age 50, 6 hours from 50 to 65, and 6.5 hours from 65 on. A tree can do this because each question is asked inside one box only.

After seven cuts, we reach the best solution (the data was simulated from it), but we can go further.

From the eighth cut on the algorithm is now splitting the noise, and the five cuts left on the slider each split off one to four subjects.
Left alone, it keeps cutting until no cut lowers the RSS, which here means until every box holds one subject.

We can also set an earlier stop by defining the minimum number of subjects per leaf.
Either way, a deep tree learns the noise of its training subjects. That's a problem.

Key idea

A tree is grown greedily: each cut is the best one available at that moment, found by trying every feature and every cutpoint. The tree as a whole is not guaranteed to be the best possible tree.

03

Pruning

Grown to the end, the tree has one subject in every leaf, and a training RSS of zero.
We know what that means: the tree overfits, it has high variance. It learned the noise of these 120 subjects, and will predict new subjects badly. 

There are two ways to keep a tree small.

Option 1, stop early: stop cutting when the best cut lowers the RSS by less than some threshold.
This is simple, but short-sighted: a cut that helps little now may open the way to a very good cut below it.

Option 2, grow and then prune: grow the full tree, then cut it back, removing the cuts that help least. This works better than option 1, usually.

To decide how far back to cut, we add a penalty for the size of the tree (regularization!):

RSS(T) + α|T|

where |T| is the number of leaves of a subtree T, and α ≥ 0 is the price of each leaf.
This is regularization again, with α in the role of λ: at α = 0 the full tree wins, and as α grows, the tree is pruned back toward its root, which predicts the mean for everyone.
Every α picks one subtree, and α is chosen by cross-validation.

Pruning the full tree
raise α and watch the boxes merge

Left: the boxes of the pruned tree.
Right: the MSE of every subtree on the way, against its number of leaves, on the 120 training subjects and on 400 new subjects simulated the same way. The slider moves along a log scale.

At α = 0 the tree has 120 leaves and a training MSE of 0, but its test MSE is 3,296.
As α grows, the smallest boxes merge first. The test MSE is lowest, 2,315, for a subtree of 8 leaves: the eight rectangles the data were simulated from.
Push α to the end, and only the root is left.

Key idea

Pruning is regularization for trees: a penalty on the number of leaves. A small α keeps a large tree with high variance, a large α a small tree with high bias.

04

Classification trees

When y is a category, a tree is grown in the same way, with two changes.

The prediction. A leaf predicts the most common class among its training subjects: a majority vote, as in the KNN classifier (class 4).
The fraction of each class in the leaf can be used as a probability.

The criterion. The RSS needs numbers to subtract, so it is replaced by a measure of how mixed a node is, its impurity.
Write pmk for the fraction of the training subjects in node m that belong to class k.
The obvious choice is the classification error rate, the fraction of the node outside its most common class:

E = 1 − maxk pmk

Surprisingly, it is not sensitive enough to grow trees with. Instead, trees use the Gini index:

G = KΣk=1 pmk(1 − pmk)

Each term is small when pmk is close to 0 or to 1, so G is small when the node is nearly pure: almost all of one class.
A split is scored by the impurity of its two nodes, each weighted by its share of the subjects.
Take a node of 100 subjects, 80 in class 0 and 20 in class 1, and a split that sets 40 of the class 0 subjects apart:

One split, two ways to score it
switch between before and after

The small tree: each box is a node, with how many of its subjects are in class 0 and in class 1, and the class it predicts.
For two classes, both measures depend only on the fraction of the node in class 1, and the curves give each measure for every fraction.
After the split, the dots at the ends of each dashed line are the two new nodes. The open circle is the node before the split, and the small dot on the dashed line under it is the split's score: the average of the two nodes, weighted by their sizes.

The split makes a pure node of 40 subjects, yet the error rate does not move: both nodes still predict class 0, so the same 20 subjects are wrong as before.
The error rate only changes when a split changes the majority of some node. A tree grown on it can get stuck: when no single split does that, every split looks worthless, even if two splits in a row would separate the classes.
The Gini index sees the progress: it falls from 0.32 to 0.27, because 40 subjects are settled.

The plot shows why. A split's score is the weighted average of its two nodes, so it lies on the straight line between them.
The error rate is made of straight lines, and when both nodes are on the same side of 0.5, the line between them is the curve itself: the score is the error rate the node had before.
The Gini curve bends, so the line between two different nodes always lies below it, and every such split lowers the Gini index.

Key idea

A classification tree predicts by a majority vote in each leaf, and is grown by lowering the Gini index. The error rate only notices a split that changes the majority of a node; the Gini index notices any step toward purer nodes.

05

Interpreting a tree on real data

Back to OASIS, the brain volume data from class 1, this time with more about each participant: their sex, education, socioeconomic status, and score on the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination), a short test of memory and thinking used to screen for dementia.
We keep the 216 participants for whom all of these were recorded, aged 33 to 96, with and without dementia, and predict brain volume from the other five features.
130 participants are the training set, and 86 the test set.

We grow the full tree on the 130, and prune it back with α chosen by 5-fold cross-validation. Then we read its questions, and test it on the other 86.

The features: age in years; sex; education, from 1 (less than high school) to 5 (beyond college); socioeconomic status (SES), from 1 (highest) to 5 (lowest); and the MMSE, from 0 to 30, where higher is better.
Bottom: every subject's predicted brain volume against their true one.

Reading the splits

The tree asks four questions, about three of the five features. From the top:

  • Age, the root: the 39 participants younger than 69 have the largest brains, 80.6% of the intracranial volume on average.
    The brain loses tissue with age, and of every cut on every feature, the best first cut is on age.
  • MMSE: among those aged 69 or more, the 14 who scored 21 or less, out of 30, have the smallest brains, 70.2% on average.
  • Age again: among the older participants with an MMSE of 22 or more, the 38 younger than 79 average 74.9%.
  • Sex: among those aged 79 or more, with an MMSE of 22 or more, the 15 men average 70.5% and the 24 women 73.1%.
    In this sample, the oldest women have more brain tissue for the size of their skull than the oldest men.

Education and socioeconomic status do not appear: once age and the MMSE have been asked, they add too little.

The tree is easy to read and explain: every question visualized and the whole model fits on one screen. 

The problem is.. it is not a very good predictor.
With 5 leaves, the tree can only predict 5 different brain volumes, so the subjects line up in 5 rows.
Its test MSE is 13.1. Least squares on the same five features reaches 10.4. The full tree, before pruning, is worse (18.5).

Key idea

A single tree is easy to read, but it predicts poorly: a few leaves can only give a few different predictions, and a large tree overfits, with very high variance. A new sample can give a very different tree.

06

Bagging

A single tree has two problems.
It overfits. A deep tree has low bias and very high variance: change a few training subjects and the first cut can change, and with it every cut below it.
It is greedy. Each cut is the best at the moment it is made, so the tree as a whole can miss a better set of cuts.

The solution is to grow many trees, and average their predictions. A model that combines many models like this is called an ensemble. It is a general concept that is relevant to any model, not only trees.

Averaging lowers variance, so many noisy predictions become one less noisy average. But for that to happen, the trees need to be different from each other.

The bootstrap

To grow many different trees we need many training sets, and we only have one.
The bootstrap is a common method for doing that (again not only for trees).
The idea: draw n subjects out of our n, at random and with replacement. Some subjects are drawn twice or more, and others not at all: on average, a bootstrap sample holds about 63% of the subjects. 
This lets us estimate variability, sampled from our own dataset.

(Side note: the bootstrap is a general tool of statistics. Its most common use is to estimate the standard error of a statistic that has no simple formula.
For example, the standard error of a mean is sd/√n, but there is no such formula for a median. So we can compute the median of each of B bootstrap samples, and take the standard deviation of those B medians)

Bagging

Bagging, short for bootstrap aggregating, grows one deep, unpruned tree on each of B bootstrap samples, and predicts with their average:

fbag(x) = 1B BΣb=1 fb(x)

where fb is the tree grown on bootstrap sample b. Each tree overfits its own sample, but each one differently, and averaging cancels some of this noise.

Let's try it on OASIS, the brain volume data from class 1, and predict brain volume from age alone. We take 60 of the 316 subjects to train on, and keep the other 256 as the test set. 
For simple visualization, we use only the age feature here, and predict brain volume.

The size of a dot is how many times that subject was drawn into the newest bootstrap sample. An empty dot was not drawn at all.
At B = 0 no sample has been drawn yet, so every subject is a ring.

At B = 0 we see only the 60 training subjects, before any sampling.
With B = 1 we see a single tree on a single bootstrap sample: a noisy line that fits its subjects.
Every new tree varies in different places, and their average smooths out as they are added.
The test MSE falls from 11.83 for the first tree to 8.60 for the average of 100, while the trees on their own score about 11.2. More trees never make bagging overfit: they only make the average steadier.

Out-of-bag error

Each bootstrap sample leaves out about a third of the subjects: the empty dots in the plot above. They were not used to grow that tree, so they can test it.
These are the out-of-bag (OOB) subjects. To estimate the error of the average, we predict each training subject using only the trees that did not draw it, and compute the MSE of those predictions.
This OOB error estimates the test error for free: it needs no test set and no cross-validation.

OOB error and test error, as trees are added
follows B on the slider above

The same trees as above. The OOB MSE uses only the 60 training subjects; the test MSE, the 256 test subjects. The dashed line is the B on the slider above.

With 100 trees, the OOB MSE is 8.77, close to the test MSE of 8.60, though it never used the test set. Each training subject was left out by about 36 of the 100 trees, and is predicted by those alone.
With only 60 subjects, the OOB error is a noisy estimate: on other random splits of OASIS it comes out above the test MSE about as often as below it.

Key idea

Bagging averages many deep trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of the training set. Each tree has low bias and high variance; their average keeps the low bias, with much less variance. The subjects each tree left out give a free estimate of the test error, the OOB error.

07

Random Forests

Bagged trees have a weakness: they look alike.
If one feature is a strong predictor, nearly every tree will cut on it first, and their predictions will rise and fall together. They are correlated...

Averaging correlated predictions helps less than averaging independent ones.
If each of B trees has variance σ2, and any two of them have correlation ρ, adding trees can only bring the variance of their average down to ρσ2, never below it (the mathematical derivation of this claim is for another time).
To go lower, we need trees that are less alike.

Decorrelating the trees

A random forest grows each tree on a bootstrap sample, as bagging does, with one change: at every split, each tree may only choose among m features, drawn at random from the p. A new draw is made at every split.
A strong feature is then left out of most splits, and other features get their chance. The trees become more different from each other, ρ falls, and so does the variance of the forest.

m = p is bagging. Common choices are m = p/3 for regression, and m = √p for classification.
A smaller m makes the trees less correlated, but also makes each tree worse, since it often cannot use the best feature. m is a hyperparameter, and can be tuned by cross-validation.

Let's see it on the OASIS data: predict brain volume from age, sex, education, SES and the MMSE, so p = 5.
For every m from 5 (bagging) down to 1, we grow a forest of 100 trees on the same 100 bootstrap samples.

The bar through a dot reaches one standard deviation of the 100 trees' predictions for that participant, above and below the forest's: the longer the bar, the more the trees disagree.

With m = 5, all 100 trees ask about age first, the strongest feature, and their predictions rise and fall together: the correlation between two trees' test predictions is 0.66, and the bars are short.

With m = 2, only 35 trees start with age. The trees disagree more: the bars grow, and the correlation falls to 0.59. Each tree on its own predicts a little worse (test MSE 18.80, against 17.75), but their average predicts better: 11.07, against 11.67 for bagging.

With m = 1, every split must take the one feature it is given, useful or not. The trees disagree the most (0.49), but each one is now so weak (21.50) that the forest gets worse again (12.21). 

Every one of these forests beats the single pruned tree of tab 05 on the same participants, whose test MSE was 13.1.

The hyperparameters of a forest

There are several parameters that can be tuned in a random forest. Three common settings from scikit-learn:

  • The number of trees, B (n_estimators): generally, the more the merrier. More trees never overfit; they only cost time.
  • The number of features to draw, m (max_features): typically √p or p/3. With m = p, the forest is bagging.
  • The minimum number of subjects per leaf, nmin (min_samples_leaf), for each tree.

m and nmin are tuned by cross-validation like any other hyperparameter. You could also use the OOB error for that.
Note: scikit-learn's RandomForestRegressor default has m=p features unless max_features is set, so out of the box it does bagging, not a random forest.

Key idea

A random forest is bagging with a random subset of m features at every split. The subsets make the trees less correlated, which lowers the part of the variance that averaging alone cannot remove.

08

Feature importance

Let's take the best forest of the previous tab: 100 trees on the OASIS data, with m = 2 of the five features to choose from at every split.
Its test MSE is 11.07: better than the single pruned tree of tab 05 on the same participants (13.1), and close to least squares on the same five features (10.4).

The price is interpretability. We could read the single tree's questions; we cannot read 100 trees.
We can still ask which features the forest relies on. There are two common measures of feature importance.

Impurity importance. Every split lowers the RSS (or the Gini index) by some amount. In each tree, we add up the decreases of every split on a feature and divide by that tree's total decrease, so each tree's importances sum to 1. Then we average over the trees.
This is scikit-learn's default, feature_importances_, and it comes free with the fit. But it is measured on the training data, and it tends to favour features with many different values.

Permutation importance. We shuffle one feature's values across the test subjects, which breaks its link with y and leaves everything else as it was, and see how much the test MSE rises.
A feature the forest depends on makes the error rise a lot. It takes more computing, but it is measured on held-out data, so it reflects prediction.

Which features does the forest use?
switch between the two measures

All five features, by each measure. The forest is the one of the previous tab with m = 2: 100 trees, 130 training and 86 test participants.

Both measures put age first, by far, and the MMSE second: the same two features the pruned tree of tab 05 asked about first.
They disagree about the other three. Impurity importance gives education (7.8%) and SES (6.5%) about twice the share of sex (3.8%). But on the test subjects, shuffling sex raises the error about as much as shuffling SES, and more than shuffling education.
Sex offers a tree only one possible cut, and education and SES four each. Impurity importance is measured on the training data, where more cuts mean more chances to lower the RSS by luck, and so it favours features with more values.

Neither measure says how a feature matters, or in which direction: an importance is not a coefficient. It only says how much the forest's predictions depend on the feature.

Key idea

A forest trades the readability of one tree for accuracy. Feature importance gives back part of it: which features the predictions depend on, though not how.

09

Glossary

Everything introduced today, on top of the glossaries from classes 1 to 5.

decision tree
A model that predicts by a series of yes-or-no questions about the features. A regression tree predicts the mean of a leaf, a classification tree its most common class.
root
The first question of a tree, which every subject passes through.
internal node
Any question below the root.
branch
One of the two answers that leave a node: "yes" to the left, "no" to the right.
leaf,
terminal node
The end of a path, where the prediction is made. Each leaf is one box of the feature space.
depth
The number of questions between the root and a leaf.
cutpoint
The value s a split compares a feature with: Xj < s or Xj ≥ s.
recursive binary splitting
Growing a tree top-down and greedily: at every step, the cut that lowers the RSS most, over every box, feature and cutpoint.
greedy
Taking the best step now, without looking ahead. Fast, but not guaranteed to find the best tree.
pruning
Growing the full tree, then cutting it back to the subtree that minimizes RSS + α|T|.
α
The price of a leaf when pruning, the tree's version of λ. α = 0 keeps the full tree. Chosen by cross-validation.
impurity
How mixed the classes in a node are. A classification tree is grown by lowering it.
classification error rate
1 − maxk pmk: the fraction of a node outside its most common class. It only changes when a split changes the majority of a node, so it is too insensitive to grow trees with.
Gini index
Σk pmk(1 − pmk): small when a node is nearly pure. The usual criterion for classification trees.
ensemble
A model that combines the predictions of many models, for example by averaging them.
bootstrap
Drawing n subjects out of n, at random with replacement. Also used to estimate the variance of a statistic.
bagging
Bootstrap aggregating: one deep tree per bootstrap sample, and the average of their predictions.
random forest
Bagging, with each split choosing among m features drawn at random. The trees are less correlated, so their average varies less.
m, max_features
How many features each split may choose from. m = p is bagging.
ρ
The correlation between two trees' predictions. Averaging cannot push the variance below ρσ2.
impurity importance
A feature's share of each tree's RSS (or Gini) decrease, averaged over the trees. scikit-learn's feature_importances_.
permutation importance
How much the test error rises when a feature's values are shuffled.
out-of-bag (OOB)
The subjects a bootstrap sample left out, about a third of them.
OOB error
The error of predicting each training subject with only the trees that did not draw it. A free estimate of the test error.
n_estimators
The number of trees, B.
min_samples_​leaf
The smallest number of training subjects a leaf may hold.
