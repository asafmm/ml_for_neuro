01

Overfitting and underfitting

In class 2 we encountered several modelling decisions: which interactions and powers to include in a linear regression?
How should we choose the best K for KNN?

In class 1, we discussed how we can fit the training data very well with a high-degree polynomial (e.g., x12), but that would not necessarily work well on unseen test data.
Let's revisit this example.
Here it is again: 25 training subjects, 40 test subjects, and the degree of the polynomial as our measure of flexibility.

One sample, fitted with more and more flexibility
slide the degree from too stiff to far too bendy

The lower plot is the same two numbers at every degree. The dashed line marks the degree you chose.

Two things we see from the plot:

  • Train error falls at every step of flexibility. It can never tell us when to stop.
  • Test error falls and then rises again. We can locate the best polynomial degree.

Here we also introduce two new terms:

  • Underfitting. High train MSE, high test MSE. The model is so simple it does not capture the relationship between the variables in either the training or the test set. MSE is high for both.
  • Overfitting. Low train MSE, high test MSE. The model is so flexible it fits the training data very well, but it fails in predicting the test set.

What causes the test MSE to have this U-shape?

02

Bias and variance

As a model gets more flexible, it can fit the data better, but it does not mean it will predict well on new data.

Imagine repeating the whole study many times: the same population, the same design, but a new sample each time. Every sample gives a slightly different fitted model. Two separate things can go wrong:

  • Bias is how far the average of those fits sits from the truth. It is the error the model makes by being the wrong shape (i.e., linear when it should be quadratic), and more data will not remove it.
  • Variance is how much the fits differ from each other given different samples (i.e., how much the model fit reacts to noise in the data).

Below, the study and the model training are repeated 30 times at whatever flexibility you choose. The measurements are noisy, as real ones are, which is what gives a flexible model something to fit.

Thirty samples, thirty fits
slide flexibility from too little to far too much

Train and test MSE at every degree, for the cohorts above. The dashed line marks your current degree, and the shaded band is the gap between the two.
Train error falls at every single degree. Nothing in it ever tells you to stop.

But why does the test error go up and the train go down? That's the bias-variance trade-off.

E[(y − f(x))2]  =  Bias(f)2  +  Var(f)  +  Var(ε)

Three terms, but we can only control the first two. The third is the irreducible noise from class 1: the part of y that no model, however good, can recover.

The two terms we control move in opposite directions: flexibility buys lower bias at the price of higher variance. The best model is not the one with the least bias, nor the one with the least variance, but the one whose sum is smallest.

The trade, drawn out
the dashed line marks your current degree

Bias falls, variance rises, their sum is U-shaped. Log scale: equal distances are equal ratios.

More subjects lower variance at every degree, so the sweet spot moves right.
Switch on the summed curve: it lands on the measured test MSE.

Key idea

A model that is too simple is wrong the same way every time. A model that is too flexible is wrong a different way every time. Both cost errors, and the cost is measured on data the model has not seen.

You cannot compute this on real data. Bias needs the truth and variance needs many samples, and in a real study we do not have this. You have one sample and the train MSE, which is the blue curve here: it falls forever and would talk you into the most flexible model on offer. A test set is what can create the red curve. The bias-variance trade-off is something we need to keep in mind when comparing and choosing between models.
03

The validation set

On the first tab we chose the degree by looking at the test MSE.
But choosing is itself a form of fitting.
We tried twelve models and kept the one that did best on these particular 40 test subjects.
Part of that success is luck: the noise in those 40 subjects happened to favor one degree over the others.

The test set was supposed to be used as the final evaluation for data the model never sees.
Once we use the test set to make a choice, it has taken part in the fitting, and the test MSE we report for the chosen model is too optimistic.

Think about it: we could compare the test MSE of 1000 models, and choose the one that gives the best test MSE.
How is that different from what we do on the training set?

The solution: another split

If we have enough data, we split it into three:

  • Training set. Fits the coefficients of every candidate model.
  • Validation set. Compares the candidates, and picks one.
  • Test set. Used once, only at the end on the chosen model: to report how well the chosen model predicts on unseen data.

A common way to do it: first set the test set aside, then split what is left 80% / 20% into training and validation.

Training, validation and test, on one study
slide the degree and watch which curve the validation MSE follows

New split keeps the same 50 subjects and only changes which 10 of them are held out for validation.
New study draws all 90 subjects again.

Look at the three curves together.
The validation MSE does not follow the train MSE down: when the model is too flexible, the MSE goes up.
It can thus help us to choose the best model.

If we do this 100 times and average the curves, we could see the convergence: the validation and test curves lie almost on top of each other, while the train curve keeps falling below both.

Key idea

Any data we use to make a choice is no longer a test. We choose with the validation set, and report with the test set, once.

Of course, there is no guarantee that the model with the lowest validation MSE would be the best for the test set, but that is our best estimation.
If you look at each individual study (press new split a few times), you see that sometimes the validation set would show a different degree.

With only 10 validation subjects, the estimate depends a lot on which subjects happened to be there.
How can we make the validation MSE more stable? The next tab discusses this.

The validation MSE is optimistic. We chose the degree because its validation MSE was the lowest, so the same optimism now applies to it. The number we report for the chosen model is its test MSE.
04

5-fold cross-validation

A single validation split has two problems, both visible on the previous tab:

  • It is noisy. The estimate depends on which subset of subjects happened to be in the validation set.
  • It is wasteful. The validation subjects never help fit the model, and with a small dataset we usually cannot spare them.

When we do not have enough data, we can re-split it into training and validation, again and again.
This is cross-validation.
We divide the data into 5 equal parts, called folds.
In each round one fold is the validation set, and the model is fitted on the other 4 folds.
After 5 rounds, every subject/sample has been in the validation set exactly once.

The MSE estimation for cross-validation is the average MSE over the folds:

MSECV = 15 5Σi=1 MSEi

MSEi is the validation MSE of round i. The cross-validation estimate is their average.

Five rounds, one estimate
step through the rounds, then change the degree

Each row of the grid is one round, and each square one subject. The column on the right is that round's validation MSE.

The test set is still held out. Cross-validation replaces only the split between training and validation.

Example: choosing K for KNN

Cross-validation is how we choose between potential models.
Here is a full example: how to choose the value of K neighbours in KNN regression.

We have a new study of 50 subjects, and six candidates: K = 1, 3, 5, 10, 20 and 40.
The procedure has three steps:

  1. Split into 5 folds, once. The folds are fixed before any model is fitted.
  2. In each fold, fit every candidate. All six models are fitted on the other four folds, and scored on the fold that was held out. One fold gives one row of validation MSE for each value of K.
  3. Average over the folds for each K, and choose. Each K gets the average of its five validation MSEs, and we choose the K with the lowest average.
MSECV(K) = 15 5Σi=1 MSEi(K)

Below, the three steps are shown together: a row for every fold, and a column for every value of K.

Choosing K with 5-fold cross-validation
click a fold to see its split, press next to fit

Why average? Look at the bold number in each row: it is the winner of that fold alone, and the folds do not agree.
Ten validation subjects make a noisy score. The average uses all 50 subjects for validation, once each, so the noise and “luck” of any single fold is cancelled.

And then? Once K is chosen, we fit it one last time on all 50 subjects, and that single model is the one we take to the test set.

05

K-fold cross-validation

Cross-validation can be done with any number of folds.
In general we cut the data into K folds, and this is called K-fold cross-validation.
Careful with the letters: this K counts folds. It has nothing to do with the K neighbours of KNN :)

In each round, one fold is the validation set, and the model is fitted on the other K−1 folds.
After K rounds, every subject has been in the validation set exactly once.

MSECV = 1K KΣi=1 MSEi

With K = 5, this is exactly the previous tab.

Choosing K for cross-validation

Common values are K = 5 and K = 10.
At the extreme, K = n: every fold holds a single subject, and each model is fitted on all the others.
This is called leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO).

As K increases, each model is trained on a larger fraction of the data.
For example, in LOO cross-validation (K = n), each model is trained on n−1 observations, compared to only n/2 when K = 2.
However, larger K values require training more models, increasing computational cost.
They also lead to greater overlap between training sets.
K = 5 and K = 10 are common values because they give a good balance between training-set size, computational cost, and overlap.

Below, the 50 subjects from the five rounds on the previous tab, with four values of K folds.
The red curve is the test MSE, measured on 1000 new subjects.
In a real study we would never have them. They are here so we can see how good each estimate is.

The same data, cut into more and more folds
the faint curves are the same estimate with the folds reshuffled

  • Small K. Each model is fitted on a smaller part of the data, so it predicts a little worse than a model fitted on all of it, and the CV MSE runs high. It also moves when we reshuffle the folds.
  • Large K. Each model is fitted on almost all of the data. The price is computation: K fits for every candidate model, so LOO needs n of them.
  • LOO has no randomness. There is only one way to leave out one subject at a time, so reshuffling changes nothing.

K = 5 or K = 10 is the usual compromise.

06

Leakage in cross-validation

Every round of cross-validation is a small train/test split: we fit on the training folds, and score on the fold held out.
The estimate is only valid if the held-out fold is new data for every step of the analysis, not only for fitting the coefficients.
So all processing of the data happens inside each round, after the split, on the training folds alone, like:

  • Model fitting: happens in each fold separately.
  • Feature selection: choosing which features go into the model.
  • Centering and scaling: subtracting each feature's mean, and dividing by its standard deviation.

When the held-out fold takes part in one of these steps, information from it reaches the model and its error does not reflect a real validation error.
This is called leakage, and it makes the CV error look better than the model really is.

Example: the effect of choosing a feature before/after the split

A typical study: 50 subjects, 1000 brain features each (say, the thickness of 1000 patches of cortex), and a behavioral score y.
With so many features, a natural first step is to keep only the one that correlates best with y, and use it to predict y.

In this simulation, none of the 1000 features has anything to do with y. They are all pure noise.

Below, the same data is analyzed in two ways:

  • Select first. We choose the feature once, on all 50 subjects, and only then split the 5 folds.
  • Split first. We split the folds first. In every round, the feature is chosen within each fold, on the 40 training subjects only.
One feature out of 1000, chosen before or after the split
switch the order, then step through the rounds

The orange frame marks the subjects the feature was chosen on. The dashed line is the MSE of predicting the mean of y for everyone.

When selecting first, the validation subjects were included in the best feature search.
The CV MSE comes out below the MSE of predicting the mean (i.e., supposedly better), as if the feature carried real information about the score.

With split first, every round makes its own choice, and often picks a different feature.
The validation subjects do not affect the selection.
The CV MSE lands near the test MSE, if anything a little above it, because each round fits on 40 subjects and not 50.
This is a valid estimate of the test MSE, because it was evaluated on truly unseen data.

Splitting by subject

Neuroscience data often comes in groups: many trials from each subject, many subjects from each recording site.
Trials from the same subject resemble each other.
If we split trials at random, every subject has trials on both sides of the split, and the model can recognize the subject instead of learning the scientifically interesting relation we are looking for.

Below we have 10 subjects with 10 trials each.
On top of a real trend, each subject has a personal level of x and a personal offset in y.
The model is KNN regression with 5 neighbours, scored by 5-fold cross-validation.

Ten subjects, ten trials each
switch how the folds are cut, then step through them

In the grid, the trials are grouped by subject, numbered left to right as the subjects sit in the plot.

Splitting by trial answers: how well do we predict a new trial, from a subject the model has already seen?
This could be a valid question, but you must decide this is what you want to test.
Splitting by subject answers another question: how well do we predict a new subject?
Each CV estimate matches the test MSE of its own question.

Most studies want the second answer, and then every trial of a subject has to land in the same fold.
The same goes for any group the model will meet as new: sessions, recording sites, scanners, etc.

Key idea

Each round of cross-validation is a train/test split. Everything the model learns from data is learned from the training folds alone, and the folds are split along the groups/structure we want to predict.

07

Nested cross-validation

So far the test set was locked away until the very end, and cross-validation only took the place of the validation set.
When the dataset is small, we often cannot afford a test set at all.
With 50 subjects, a test set of 10 gives a noisy answer, and leaves only 40 to build the model with.

To solve this, cross-validation itself often becomes the final evaluation: its error is the number we report.
But in the KNN example, the same CV error was also how we chose K.
We already showed that choosing is a form of fitting. Once the folds have chosen K, they give an optimistic result, just like a test set we chose with.
So if cross-validation is our evaluation, how do we choose the model?

The solution is nested cross-validation: cross-validation within cross-validation.

  • The outer loop evaluates. It splits the data into 5 outer folds, and each outer fold takes a turn as the test set. An outer fold never takes part in any choice.
  • The inner loop chooses. Inside each outer round, the other 40 subjects are split again, into 4 inner folds. An ordinary cross-validation on those 40 chooses K, exactly as in the KNN example.
  • Then we test. The chosen K is fitted on all 40, and scored on the outer fold.
MSEnested = 15 5Σi=1 MSEi(Ki)

Ki is the K chosen by the inner loop of round i.
MSEi is its test MSE, on outer fold i.

Below, a new study of 50 subjects, and the same candidates as in the KNN example, with one change: inside an outer round each inner fit has only 30 subjects, so the largest candidate is K = 30 instead of 40.

Nested cross-validation, one outer fold at a time
click an outer fold to see its split, press next to run it

A different K in different rounds? That is fine. Nested cross-validation does not choose K.
It scores the whole procedure: choose K by cross-validation, fit it, predict.
The choice is part of what is being tested, so it is repeated inside every round, on that round's training subjects only.

And the final model? We run the procedure once more, on all 50 subjects: a 5-fold cross-validation chooses K, as in the KNN example, and we fit it on all 50.
The nested CV MSE is our estimate of how well that model will predict new subjects.

The process of nested CV is computationally expensive: 5 outer rounds × 4 inner folds × 6 candidates = 120 fits, plus 5 refits, for one model's estimate.
It might take a bit of time, but with small datasets this is usually our best option, and the most accurate estimate for the model's performance.

Key idea

Nested CV has two loops: the inner loop chooses, the outer loop evaluates. An outer fold never takes part in any choice, so, like a test set, it can report the error honestly.
The result of the nested CV is the test MSE estimate for the whole procedure.

08

Glossary

Everything introduced today, on top of the class 1 and class 2 glossaries.

training set
The data the coefficients are fitted on.
validation set
Data held out from fitting, used to compare candidate models and pick one.
test set
Data locked away until the end, and used once: to report how well the chosen model predicts.
validation set
approach
One split into training and validation. Simple, but noisy, and it fits on only part of the data.
cross-validation
Re-splitting the same data into training and validation many times, and averaging the validation errors.
fold
One of the K equal parts the data is divided into. Each fold is the validation set in exactly one round.
K-fold CV
K rounds, each fitting on K−1 folds and validating on the one left out. Common values are K = 5 and K = 10.
MSECV
The average of the K validation MSEs. The number we compare models by.
leave-one-out
(LOO)
K = n. Every subject is its own fold. No randomness, but n fits for every candidate model.
leakage
Information from the held-out fold reaching the model by a side door, for example through feature selection done before the split. It makes the CV error too optimistic.
feature selection
Choosing which features go into the model. A form of fitting, so it is done inside each round, on the training folds.
centering and scaling
Subtracting a feature's mean and dividing by its standard deviation. Both numbers come from the training folds, and are applied unchanged to the held-out fold.
splitting by subject
Keeping every trial of a subject in the same fold, so the CV error is the error for new subjects, not for new trials of known ones.
nested CV
Cross-validation within cross-validation, for when there is no test set. The inner loop chooses the model, the outer loop estimates its error.
outer and
inner folds
An outer fold is the test set of one outer round, and takes part in no choice. The inner folds are cut from the rest, to choose with.
