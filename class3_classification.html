01

Linear vs. logistic regression

Everything so far assumed y was a number: firing rate, wage, memory score.
Plenty of the questions we actually ask are not: male or female, which of three diagnoses. 
When y is a category, the task is classification (instead of regression).

Start with the simplest case, two categories, and turn them into numbers so we can compute with them:

yi = 0control 1MDD

Why not use linear regression for classification?

Below, a connectivity score from a resting-state scan, and whether that subject met criteria for MDD. Fit a straight line to the 0/1 outcome and read it as a probability:

A straight line through a 0/1 outcome
the dashed rails are the only two values y can take

Switch on the logistic fit to see what we are heading for. It bends where the line cannot, and it never leaves the interval.

A probability below zero? When the models predicts a "probability" below 0, or above 1, it shows its structure is problematic. The straight line is unrestricted, and doesn't know that y only takes two values, so it extrapolates beyond these values. 
We need a model whose output is a probability by construction.

The logistic function

We want a model that takes any number and returns something between 0 and 1.
The linear part stays exactly as it was. We just push it through a function that squashes:

p(X) = eβ0+β1X 1 + eβ0+β1X

Read it in two halves. β0 + β1X is the same linear model as always, free to run from minus infinity to plus infinity. The fraction takes that number and bends it into (0, 1).
Large and positive goes to 1. Large and negative goes to 0. Zero goes to exactly one half.

The two coefficients do different jobs, and it is worth separating them:

  • β1 sets how steep the transition is. Bigger means a sharper switch from one class to the other.
  • β0 slides the whole curve left or right. The halfway point sits at X = −β0/β1.
What the two coefficients do
drag them and watch the curve, never the rails

Push β1 negative and the curve runs the other way: high scores become evidence against the diagnosis. Set it to zero and the model gives every subject the same probability, whatever we measured.

Where the coefficients come from

Least squares will not choose them for us. Squared error asks how far the prediction is from y, and here y is 0 or 1 while the prediction is a probability.

Instead we use maximum likelihood: pick the β that make the diagnoses we actually observed most probable. Every patient contributes the probability the model gave them, every control contributes one minus it, and we maximise the product. In practice we maximise its log, which turns that product into a sum and stops hundreds of numbers below 1 from underflowing to zero.

Two practical consequences, both worth knowing before trusting a printout:

  • There is no closed form. The β sit inside an exponential, so unlike the slope formula from class 1 the answer has to be found by iterating. Every package does this for us, and it usually settles in a handful of steps.
  • If some value of X splits the two classes cleanly, called perfect separation, there is no maximum to find: the likelihood keeps rising as β1 grows without limit. The tell is a coefficient in the thousands, and it happens most in small samples with a strong predictor.
Key idea

Nothing about the linear model was thrown away. β0 + β1X is still in there, doing exactly what it always did. All we added was a function around it, so that whatever it returns comes out as a probability.

02

The logit

The same model, on a straight axis

The curve is awkward to reason about, so rearrange it. Take the equation, divide, and the exponential comes out on its own:

p(X)1 − p(X)  =  eβ0+β1X

That ratio is the odds: how many times more likely the diagnosis is than its absence. A probability of 0.8 is odds of 4, or four to one.
Take the log of both sides and the right-hand side is bare:

log p(X)1 − p(X)  =  β0 + β1X

The left side is the logit, or the log odds. On that scale the model is a straight line, with exactly the reading we are used to: one more unit of X adds β1 to the log odds, everywhere along the axis.

Both panels below are the same model. Only the vertical axis differs.

Probability on the left, log odds on the right
one model, two scales

Probability. Bounded, and the effect of X changes along the axis: steep in the middle, almost flat at either end.

Log odds. Unbounded, and the effect of X is one constant slope. Same β1 as the panel above.

So what does β1 actually mean?

In linear regression the answer was easy: one more unit of X adds β1 to y, and that was the end of it.

Here the same question has three answers, depending on which scale we ask it on. Take one step along X, from wherever we happen to be standing, and:

  • on the logit scale the step adds β1, always, wherever we started;
  • on the odds scale it multiplies by eβ1, always, wherever we started;
  • on the probability scale it adds an amount that depends entirely on where we started.

Move the step along the axis below and watch which of the three readings holds still.

One step in X, read on three scales
slide the step along and see what stays constant

Log odds. The step is the same height anywhere on the axis.

Probability. The same step, and it shrinks toward both ends.

The step is always one unit of X wide. Only where it sits changes.
Set β1 to zero and all three readings go flat: the predictor is telling us nothing.

Key idea

Report eβ1, the odds ratio, not β1 itself and not a change in probability. It is the one summary of the coefficient that is true everywhere along the axis, and it is what the clinical literature expects.

β1 is not a change in probability. It is a change in log odds, and the same β1 buys a different amount of probability depending on where we start. Moving a subject from 0.50 to 0.62 and from 0.95 to 0.96 can both be the same step in β1. Exponentiate it, eβ1, and we get an odds ratio, which at least multiplies consistently.
And the reason all of this matters

Logistic regression is still a linear model. It is linear in the log odds, not in the probability. Just like linear regression, we can still add multiple predictors, powers, dummies, and interactions.

03

The decision boundary

One predictor gave us a curve. Two predictors give us something we can actually see a classifier do.

Nothing changes except the length of the linear part:

log p1 − p  =  β0 + β1x1 + β2x2

The model still returns a probability for every subject. To turn that into a decision we need a rule, and the usual one is: call it MDD when p ≥ 0.5.

Now ask where the decision changes. p = 0.5 means odds of 1, and log 1 = 0, so the switch happens exactly where the linear part is zero:

β0 + β1x1 + β2x2  =  0

That is the equation of a straight line. Rearranged the way we would plot it:

x2 = − β0 + β1x1 β2

This line is the decision boundary: every subject on one side is called a patient, every subject on the other a control. With three predictors it becomes a flat plane, and with p of them a hyperplane. Logistic regression is a linear classifier for exactly this reason.

Two connectivity scores below, from two different networks, and the same binary outcome.

Two predictors, one straight boundary
move the coefficients and watch the line, then fit it

The shading is the fitted probability everywhere in the plane, not just where we have subjects. The solid line is where it passes 0.5, and the two dashed lines mark 0.25 and 0.75.

Three things are worth reading off that panel.

  • β0 slides the boundary without turning it. It is the intercept, doing here exactly what it did in one dimension.
  • β1 and β2 together set its angle. Only their ratio matters for where the line sits: doubling both leaves the boundary alone.
  • Doubling both does change the shading. The bigger the coefficients, the faster the probability swings from 0 to 1 as we cross, so the dashed lines close in. That is the same steepness β1 controlled on the curve in section 1.

So the coefficients carry two separate pieces of information: a direction, which fixes the boundary, and a magnitude, which fixes how confident the model is near it.

One thing the panel will show if you hunt for it: the fitted line is not quite the most accurate line. Maximum likelihood is chasing the probabilities, not the count of correct calls, and nudging the sliders away from the fit can win a subject or two. That is a feature, not a bug, and the tab on error metrics takes it apart: maximum likelihood is estimating probabilities, and nobody told it we were going to threshold them at one half.

Key idea

The probability surface is curved, but the boundary it draws is flat. Every subject with the same value of β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 gets the same prediction, whatever combination of scores produced it.

Straight in the columns we supplied, not in the measurements. If the two groups are separated by a ring rather than a line, no choice of β will find it. The fix is the one from class 2: add x12, x22 and x1x2 as columns of their own, and the boundary becomes a conic in the original two scores while the model stays linear in its coefficients. We come back to what happens when we do not in the last tab.
04

More than two classes

Everything so far had two categories. Plenty of questions have more: three diagnoses, four task conditions, six stimulus categories.

The tempting move is to number them and carry on, exactly as we did with 0 and 1:

yi = 0healthy 1MDD 2schizophrenia

With two categories that was harmless: any 0/1 coding gives the same fit, flipped at worst.
With three it says two things we never meant to say. It says schizophrenia is twice MDD, and it says the step from healthy to MDD is the same size as the step from MDD to schizophrenia. Neither is a claim about the brain, and both are claims the model will happily fit.

The fix keeps the structure and drops the ordering. Pick one category as the baseline, exactly as we did with dummies in class 2, and fit a separate set of coefficients for each of the others. K categories need K − 1 sets.

p(Y=m | X) = eβm0+βm1X 1 + K−1Σc=1 eβc0+βc1X

The baseline gets no coefficients of its own. Its probability is whatever is left over:

p(Y=K | X) = 1 1 + K−1Σc=1 eβc0+βc1X

The denominator is shared, which is what forces the K probabilities to sum to 1 at every value of X. No category can quietly go missing.

And the log odds still come out straight, now as a comparison against the baseline:

log p(Y=m | X)p(Y=K | X)  =  βm0 + βm1X
Three diagnoses, one predictor
change the baseline and watch what does not move

The three curves never move when the baseline changes. The coefficients do, and so does what they mean: each one is now a comparison against a different reference group.

Key idea

The choice of baseline is a choice about reporting, not about the model. Predictions are identical either way. Pick the group a reader will want everything compared against, usually the controls.

Coefficients are unreadable without their baseline. “β1 = 0.8 for MDD” means nothing on its own. Against healthy controls it is one claim, against schizophrenia a different one, and the same fitted model produces both numbers. Always report which group is the reference.
05

Error types and metrics

The model returns a probability. A decision needs a class, so somewhere we have to pick a threshold and call everything above it a patient.

One half is the obvious choice, and it is only a choice. It is almost never the right one.

Once we threshold, every subject lands in one of four boxes, and the two kinds of mistake are not the same mistake:

acc = 1n nΣi=1 𝕀(yi = yi)

Accuracy counts all four boxes as though they were interchangeable. In class 1 we saw what that costs when one group is rare. Here is the rest of the problem: even with balanced groups, accuracy cannot tell a missed patient from a false alarm, and those have different consequences in every clinic we might work in.

Move the threshold, move the errors
the two mistakes trade against each other, always

Drag the threshold to 0 and we call everyone a patient: every patient found, every control alarmed. Drag it to 1 and the reverse. Everything useful happens in between.

Four numbers worth knowing

Each one divides a box by a row or a column of the table above, and each answers a different question:

  • Sensitivity, or recall: of the patients, how many did we find? Divides by the patient row. This is the one that matters when missing a case is dangerous.
  • Specificity: of the controls, how many did we leave alone? Divides by the control row.
  • Precision: of those we flagged, how many really were patients? Divides by the flagged column, so unlike the other two it moves when prevalence moves.
  • F1: the harmonic mean of precision and recall, for when we want one number and accuracy will not do.

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test. Precision is a property of the test and the population we ran it in, which is why a screening tool that looked excellent in a patient-heavy validation set disappoints in the general clinic.

Every threshold at once
the ROC curve, and the area under it

Each point on the curve is one threshold, sweeping from “flag nobody” at the bottom left to “flag everybody” at the top right. The dot is the threshold set in the panel above.
The diagonal is a coin flip. Area 0.5 means the score carries no information at all.

The fit moves the metrics too

Everything so far held the model still and moved the threshold. Now do the opposite: hold the threshold at one half and move the model.

These are 400 fresh subjects from a screening setting, so only about a third of them have the diagnosis.

With one predictor the rule p ≥ 0.5 is the rule β0 + β1x ≥ 0, which is the rule x ≥ −β0/β1. So every threshold metric on this page depends on the two coefficients through a single number: the score at which the model changes its mind.

Same subjects, different coefficients
the threshold is pinned at 0.5 throughout

Two different jobs. “Best fit” chooses the coefficients that make the observed diagnoses most probable. “Best accuracy” chooses the cut that gets the most subjects on the right side. They are not the same coefficients, and usually not the same cut.

Three things worth pulling out of that panel.

  • Many coefficients, one answer. Double both β and every count in the table is unchanged, because −β0/β1 is unchanged. The probabilities all moved, the decisions did not.
  • AUC does not move at all, for any β1 > 0. It only scores the ordering of the subjects, and with one predictor a positive β1 always gives the same ordering. Flip β1 negative and it becomes 1 − AUC, because the ordering reverses.
  • The best-fitting model is not the most accurate one. Press the two buttons in turn: the fit gets 82.8% right, and simply sliding the cut left gets 84.5% while raising sensitivity from 69% to 79%. Maximum likelihood is estimating probabilities, and nobody told it we were going to threshold them at one half.
Key idea

AUC scores the ranking, not the decision. It asks: take one random patient and one random control, how often does the model give the patient the higher score? That is useful precisely because it does not depend on a threshold, and useless the moment we have to actually choose one.

Report the threshold with the metric. Sensitivity of 0.92 is meaningless without saying where we cut, because we can always buy sensitivity with specificity by moving the cut. Two papers reporting different sensitivities for the same model are often reporting the same curve at two different points on it.
06

The KNN classifier

Class 2 used nearest neighbours for regression: find the K closest training points and average their y.
For classification, change the last word. Find the K closest points and take a vote.

p(Y=j | X=x0) = 1K Σi ∈ N0 𝕀(yi = j)

The fraction of the neighbourhood belonging to class j, used as a probability. Whichever class wins the vote is the prediction.

Still no training step, still nothing to solve for. And now the difference from logistic regression is visible in a way it was not with regression, because a classifier draws a decision boundary: the line where the prediction flips.

Two boundaries on the same data
one is a straight line by construction, the other is whatever the data says

The dashed line is the Bayes boundary: where the true probability is exactly one half. It is the best any classifier could do, and we only know it because the data is simulated.

Set the truth to linear and logistic regression wins, for the same reason it won in class 2: it was handed the right shape and spends all its data on two coefficients. Set it to curved and no straight line can follow, however much data we give it.

At K = 1 the boundary is a jagged outline that separates the training points perfectly and has learned the noise. Raising K smooths it toward the truth, then past it.

The same U, in error rate
flexibility runs right to left, as 1/K

By convention the axis is 1/K, so flexibility increases to the right and the picture matches the bias-variance curve from class 2.
At K = 1 the training error is exactly zero: every point is its own nearest neighbour, so it always votes for itself. That number tells us nothing whatsoever.

Key idea

Same trade as class 2, same shape of curve, different error measure. Nothing about the bias-variance tradeoff was specific to regression: it is a statement about flexibility, and it applies to any model we fit.

Scale your features first. Nearest means Euclidean distance, so a predictor measured in thousands dominates one measured in tenths, whatever either one actually tells us. Logistic regression is immune to this, because a rescaled column just rescales its coefficient. KNN is not, and standardising the columns is not optional.
07

Glossary

Everything introduced today, on top of the class 1 and class 2 glossaries.

classification
Supervised learning where y is a category rather than a number.
logistic function
ez/(1+ez). Takes any number and returns one strictly between 0 and 1.
odds
p/(1−p). How many times more likely the outcome is than its absence.
logit
log(p/(1−p)), the log odds. The scale on which logistic regression is a straight line.
odds ratio
eβ1. What the odds are multiplied by for one more unit of X.
likelihood
The probability of the data we collected, read as a function of the coefficients.
log-likelihood
Its log, which turns the product into a sum. What the optimiser actually maximises.
maximum
likelihood
Choosing the coefficients that make the observed data most probable.
no closed form
The score equations cannot be solved algebraically, so the fit is found by iterating.
perfect separation
Some value of X splits the classes cleanly, so the likelihood has no maximum and the coefficients diverge.
multinomial
Logistic regression for K > 2 categories, with K − 1 coefficient sets against a baseline.
threshold
The probability above which we call a case positive. A decision we make, not one the model makes.
confusion matrix
The four counts: true and false positives, true and false negatives.
sensitivity
(recall)
Of the true positives, the fraction we found. TP/(TP+FN).
specificity
Of the true negatives, the fraction we left alone. TN/(TN+FP).
precision
Of those we flagged, the fraction that were real. TP/(TP+FP). Depends on prevalence.
F1
Harmonic mean of precision and recall, when one number is wanted.
ROC curve
Sensitivity against 1 − specificity, traced across every threshold.
AUC
Area under that curve. The chance a random positive outranks a random negative.
decision boundary
The set of points where the prediction flips from one class to another.
Bayes boundary
Where the true probability is exactly one half. The best boundary any classifier could draw.
KNN classifier
Predict the class by majority vote among the K nearest training points.
