Least Squares

The simplest guess at f is a straight line. Where that line comes from, how good it is, and why the number you report depends on which data you computed it on.

Dr. Asaf Madar · lecturer

RSS shrinking onto the fit
01

The linear model

The simplest possible guess at f is a straight line. Assume y changes by a constant amount for every unit of x:

y ≈ β0 + β1x

Two numbers, and they are both readable in plain language.

  • β0, the intercept: the predicted y when x = 0. Here that is the recall score of an average-sized hippocampus.
  • β1, the slope: how much y moves per one-unit increase in x. This is the number you interpret.

Note that the slope turns the line about the point x = 0, marked on the plot. That is the only point the slope leaves alone, which is why a predictor is often measured from its own mean: it puts the pivot in the middle of the data instead of far outside it.

“Fitting the model” means nothing more than choosing values for these two. Try it by hand, you are the optimiser:

02

Least squares

You just moved a line around until it “looked right”. To do that automatically we need to say precisely what “right” means.

For each subject, the model predicts yi = β0 + β1xi, and reality delivered yi. The gap between them is the residual, ei = yi − yi — the vertical distance from the point to the line.

We cannot just add the residuals up: the positive and negative ones cancel, and a terrible line can score zero. So we square each one first, then add. That is the residual sum of squares:

RSS = nΣi=1 (yi − yi)2

Squaring does two things. It makes every error count as positive, and it punishes large misses disproportionately — being off by 4 is sixteen times worse than being off by 1, not four times worse. Ordinary least squares (OLS) is simply the choice of β0 and β1 that makes this number as small as it can be.

Every residual is drawn below as a red stick running from the observation to the line. Making the line better means making those sticks shorter, all of them at once:

Each stick is one residual. RSS squares every length and adds them up, so the long sticks dominate the total. Least squares is the line that leaves the smallest total.

03

Solving it with calculus

Hunting for the best line by hand is fine for two parameters, but we can do better.
Think of RSS as a function of β. It is a parabola: steep where the line is badly wrong, flattening out as it improves, and perfectly flat at its lowest point.
Flat means the derivative is zero, and that is the whole trick: write RSS as a function of β, differentiate, set it to zero, and solve.

To keep the algebra visible we drop the intercept for a moment and fit a line through the origin, y = βx, so there is only one unknown. Reveal one step at a time:

01
RSS = f(β) = nΣi=1 (yi − βxi)2
The data xi and yi are fixed numbers you already measured. The only thing that can vary is β. So RSS is an ordinary function of one variable — and we want its minimum.
02
f′(β) = −2 nΣi=1 (yi − βxi) xi
Differentiate with respect to β. Each term is something-squared, so the chain rule gives 2·(inside)·(derivative of inside), and the derivative of (yi − βxi) with respect to β is just −xi.
03
−2 nΣi=1 (yixi − βxi2) = 0
Set the derivative to zero — this is the definition of the minimum — and multiply out the bracket. The −2 in front now does nothing, since zero divided by −2 is still zero.
04
nΣi=1 yixi  =  β nΣi=1 xi2
Split the sum in two and move one side across. β is a single number, not indexed by i, so it comes out in front of the sum.
05
β = nΣi=1 yixi nΣi=1 xi2
Divide. That is it — a closed-form answer. No search, no iteration, no learning rate. Two sums over your data and you have the optimal slope exactly.

Putting the intercept back

Doing the same two-variable version — take the partial derivative with respect to β0 and with respect to β1, set both to zero, solve the pair — gives the formulas you will actually use:

β1 = nΣi=1(xi − x)(yi − y) nΣi=1(xi − x)2 β0 = y − β1x

Same structure as step 05, with every variable measured from its own mean instead of from zero. Two things fall straight out of these, and both are worth remembering.

First, β0 = y − β1x rearranges to say that the fitted line always passes exactly through the point (x, y). Second, that numerator is the covariance of x and y and the denominator is the variance of x, which means:

β1 = r · sysx
Key idea

The regression slope is the correlation, rescaled by the spread of the two variables. Correlation and simple regression are the same fact wearing different units — which is exactly why a strong p-value on a slope tells you nothing about whether the model can predict.

04

How good is the fit?

RSS on its own is unreadable. Is 4,300 good? It depends entirely on the units of y and on how many subjects you have. Two fixes.

MSE divides RSS by n, so at least it does not grow just because you collected more data. Its square root, RMSE, is back in the original units — “on average we miss by 3.4 points” is a sentence a clinician can use.

R2 takes a different route: it compares your model to the laziest possible one. If you had no x at all, your best guess for everybody would be the mean y. The error of that null model is the total sum of squares, TSS. R2 asks what fraction of it your line removed.

R2 = 1 − RSSTSS TSS = Σ(yi − y)2

R2 = 0 means the line did no better than guessing the mean every time. R2 = 1 means the residuals vanished. Toggle between the two models below and watch the sticks shorten: the fraction of the total you removed is R2.

R2 never goes down when you add a predictor. Add pure random noise as a variable and R2 creeps up, because the extra freedom lets the model chase the noise. A high in-sample R2 is not evidence of anything until you have checked it out of sample.
05

Noisy data, small samples

Everything so far works beautifully with 500 clean observations. Neuroscience rarely offers those. You get 25 subjects, an effect that explains a few percent of the variance, and a hundred candidate predictors.

Test R2 can be negative. That is not a bug: it means the model predicts new subjects worse than simply guessing the training mean would have.

Two things to notice as you click through cohorts. At n = 20 the estimated slope swings wildly around the true one — each cohort tells a different story, and any single published one might be yours. And train R2 is systematically optimistic: it is computed on the very points that were used to place the line, so it gets to peek at the answer.

Drag n up to 200 and both problems fade. That gap between the two numbers is the whole reason this course insists on a test set.

Key idea

A metric computed on the data you fitted is a description. The same metric computed on data the model has never seen is a prediction. Only the second one generalises — and it is the only one anyone should believe.

What this looks like in Python

Everything in this class is four lines of scikit-learn. Which is exactly the danger: the library will happily let you skip the split.

from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split from sklearn.metrics import r2_score, mean_squared_error X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=0) model = LinearRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr) model.intercept_ # beta_0 model.coef_ # beta_1 ... beta_p r2_score(y_tr, model.predict(X_tr)) # the flattering number r2_score(y_te, model.predict(X_te)) # the honest one

Glossary

Everything introduced today, in the notation the course will keep using.

x
Feature / predictor. What you measured and are willing to use as input.
y
Outcome / target. What you want the model to produce.
f
The true relationship between them. Unknown, and stays unknown.
f
Your estimate of it — the fitted model. The hat means “estimated from data”.
ε
Irreducible noise. The part of y no model can ever recover.
yi
The model’s prediction for subject i.
n / p
Number of observations / number of features. In neuroscience p > n is normal, and it breaks things.
residual
ei = yi − yi. The vertical miss for one observation.
RSS
Sum of squared residuals. What least squares minimises.
TSS
Sum of squared deviations from y. The error of the null model.
MSE
RSS divided by n. The standard regression loss.
RMSE
Square root of MSE, back in the units of y.
R2
1 − RSS/TSS. Fraction of variance in y the model accounts for.
β0, β1
Intercept and slope. The weights the model learns.
OLS
Ordinary least squares. The recipe for finding those weights.
training set
The data used to choose the weights.
test set
Data held back and touched exactly once, to estimate real performance.
overfitting
Fitting the noise. Train error falls, test error rises.
supervised
Learning with labelled y. Regression or classification.
unsupervised
Structure-finding with no y. Clustering, dimensionality reduction.
accuracy
Proportion of correct classifications. Misleading when classes are imbalanced.
recall
Of the true positives, the fraction the model found.
