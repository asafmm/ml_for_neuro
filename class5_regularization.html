Shrink to
Fit

With hundreds of brain features and a few dozen subjects, least squares fits the training set perfectly and predicts badly. Two ways out: keep fewer features, or keep them all and pull their coefficients toward zero.

Asaf Madar, PhD · lecturer

12 coefficients · leaving zero one at a time
01

More features than subjects

In neuroscience we often measure many more features than we have subjects:
the thickness of hundreds of cortical regions, the activity of thousands of voxels, the connectivity between every pair of regions.
We write this as p ≫ n: the number of features p is much larger than the number of subjects n.

Today's data is real. ABIDE II is an open MRI dataset of 1004 children and adults, aged 5 to 64, scanned at 17 sites.
For every participant, the thickness of the cortex was measured in 360 regions.
We will try to predict age from these 360 numbers. The cortex gets thinner as we grow up, so there is something to find.

A typical study has a few dozen subjects, so we draw 60 participants to train on, and keep 200 others as a test set.
That is a table of n = 60 rows and p = 360 features, for the linear model from class 1:

yi ≈ β0 + β1xi1 + β2xi2 + … + βpxip  =  β0 + pΣj=1 βjxij

Count the unknowns: one intercept and 360 slopes, 361 numbers to estimate from 60 subjects.
Once the model has as many numbers as there are subjects, least squares can pass exactly through every training point, and the training error is zero.
With even more features, infinitely many sets of coefficients fit perfectly, and least squares no longer has a single answer.

Is this a bias problem or a variance problem?
Let's watch it happen. Below, least squares uses the first k regions in a fixed random order, from 1 up to 59.

Least squares on more and more regions
slide k all the way to 59

Left: every subject's predicted age against their true age. A prediction beyond the frame is drawn on its edge.
Right: the MSE of the same fit, for every k.

The training MSE only goes down. Every new region gives least squares more freedom to fit these 60 subjects, noise included.
At 59 regions plus the intercept there are as many numbers as subjects, and the fit is perfect.

The test MSE goes down at first, while the new regions still carry information about age, and then it goes up. Close to 59 it explodes.
So it is a variance problem: with this many coefficients, the fit follows the noise of the particular 60 subjects we happened to draw.

With p ≫ n we cannot simply use every feature. We need to decide which features go into the model, and how many.

Key idea

Once a model has as many coefficients as there are subjects, a perfect fit to the training data is guaranteed, and it tells us nothing about prediction. Only held-out data can show how many features actually help.

02

Selecting features by a criterion

The simplest way to choose: score every feature on its own, by how strongly it is related to y, and keep the best ones.

  1. Calculate a criterion c(xj, y) for every feature xj.
  2. Keep the k features with the best c.

A common criterion is the correlation r between the feature and y.
A t-statistic or an F-statistic of each feature on its own ranks the features in exactly the same order.
Here we use the correlation with age, computed on the 60 training subjects.

How many features should we keep? k is a setting like the polynomial degree, or the number of neighbours in KNN, so we choose it the same way: by cross-validation (class 3).

Keep the regions most correlated with age
slide k, then let cross-validation choose

Top: the correlation of each region with age on the 60 training subjects, strongest first. The orange bars are the ones kept.
Each CV round ranks the regions again, on its own 48 training subjects, so it can keep different ones.

Cross-validation chooses k = 14: the 14 regions most correlated with age.
Their test MSE is 44.7, against 49.6 for the best number of regions in the random order of the previous tab, and 78.6 for predicting the mean age for everyone.

A few strong regions already predict well. Beyond them, every region added brings more noise than information.

Select inside the folds. Choosing features is part of fitting the model. In every round of cross-validation, the correlations are computed on that round's training folds only, and the held-out fold has no say in which regions are kept. Ranking the regions once on all 60 subjects, and only then splitting the folds, would leak the validation subjects into the choice and make the CV error look better than it is.

What this kind of selection cannot do

The criterion looks at one feature at a time. It cannot tell when two features carry the same information, like neighbouring regions, or the same region on the left and on the right.
And every feature is either fully in the model or fully out of it.

The next tabs take a different route: keep every feature, and let the fit itself decide how much each one counts.

Key idea

Selecting features is part of fitting. The number of features is chosen by cross-validation, and the selection itself is repeated inside every fold, on the training folds alone.

03

Ridge regression

Feature selection makes a hard decision for every feature: in or out.
Regularization keeps all the features, and limits how large their coefficients can grow instead.
It works as an automatic feature selection: the fit itself decides how much each feature counts.

Standard linear regression minimizes the RSS:

RSS(β) = nΣi=1 (yi − β0 − pΣj=1 βjxij)2

Ridge regression adds the size of the coefficients to it, as a penalty:

L(β, λ) = RSS(β) + λ pΣj=1 βj2

The first part is the RSS we know. The second is the penalty: the sum of the squared coefficients, multiplied by λ ≥ 0, the regularization parameter.
The sum starts at j = 1: the intercept β0 is not penalized, because it only sets the average level of y.

This penalty is called the L2 penalty, and ridge is also called L2 regularization.
The name comes from the L2 norm, the ordinary length of the list of coefficients, measured with Pythagoras:

L2 norm = √(β12 + β22 + … + βp2)

The ridge penalty is λ times this length squared: the larger the coefficients, the longer the length, and the more it costs.

λ sets the price of a large coefficient:

  • λ = 0: the penalty disappears, and ridge regression is standard linear regression.
  • λ → ∞: any coefficient other than 0 costs too much, so every βj = 0. This is the null model, which predicts y for everyone.
  • In between, the coefficients are pulled toward zero. We say they are shrunk.

The same loss as a constraint

There is a second way to write ridge regression. Instead of adding a penalty, keep the RSS alone, and limit how large the coefficients may be:

minimize  nΣi=1 (yi − β0 − pΣj=1 βjxij)2  subject to  pΣj=1 βj2 ≤ s

The two forms give the same coefficients. Every λ matches one limit s in the constraint: the larger λ, the smaller s, and the tighter the constraint.

The constrained form can be drawn. Take only two of the 360 regions: IP0, a region in the intraparietal sulcus, in the left hemisphere (x1) and in the right (x2), on the same 60 subjects.
Both are measured in standard deviations from their mean, for a reason we will get to below.
The red ellipses are contours of the RSS: the least-squares loss surface seen from above, as in class 1, with its lowest point at least squares. The shading and the number on each ellipse give the RSS there: the deeper the shade, the lower the RSS.
The teal disk is the constraint region, β12 + β22 ≤ s: every pair of coefficients whose L2 length is at most √s.
Ridge picks the point of the disk with the lowest RSS: where the smallest ellipse that reaches the disk touches it.

At λ = 0 the constraint region is large enough to hold least squares itself, and ridge is least squares.
As λ grows, the disk shrinks, and the ridge solution slides along its edge toward (0, 0).
A circle has no corners, so the ellipse touches it at a point where both coefficients are non-zero: ridge shrinks every coefficient, but sets none to exactly zero.

Notice the direction it takes. The left and right IP0 are correlated (r = 0.65), so the ellipses are long and tilted: many pairs of coefficients predict almost equally well.
Ridge pulls the two coefficients toward each other as well as toward zero, so the weight ends up shared between the two regions.
When features carry the same information, ridge spreads the weight across them.

In matrix form

With all the features in a matrix X (n rows, p columns) and the outcomes in a column y, the two losses are:

RSS(β) = (y − Xβ)T(y − Xβ) L(β, λ) = (y − Xβ)T(y − Xβ) + λβTβ

Setting the derivative to zero, as in class 1, gives each solution in one line:

βOLS = (XTX)−1XTy βridge = (XTX + λI)−1XTy

Ridge adds λ to the diagonal of XTX (I is the identity matrix).
When p > n, XTX cannot be inverted: that is the matrix version of "infinitely many perfect fits".
XTX + λI can always be inverted when λ > 0, so ridge has exactly one answer, even with 360 regions and 60 subjects.

Scale the features first

The penalty adds up βj2 over all the features, so the units of each feature matter.
If one region's thickness were recorded in micrometres instead of millimetres, its values would be 1000 times larger, its coefficient 1000 times smaller, and its penalty a million times smaller.
Features measured on large scales would be almost free to use, and features measured on small scales would be punished.

So before fitting, we put every feature on the same scale by standardizing it:

x → x − xsd(x)

Every feature then has mean 0 and standard deviation 1, and every coefficient is in the same units: the change in y for one standard deviation of the feature.

Scaling is fitted, too. The mean and the standard deviation come from the training set only, and inside cross-validation from the training folds only. The held-out subjects are scaled with those same numbers (class 3).
Key idea

Ridge keeps every feature and shrinks all the coefficients toward zero. λ = 0 is least squares, λ → ∞ is the null model, and the useful models are in between.

04

Lasso regression

Lasso stands for Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator.
It changes one thing in the ridge loss: the penalty adds up the absolute values of the coefficients, instead of their squares.

ridge:  L(β, λ) = RSS + λ pΣj=1 βj2 lasso:  L(β, λ) = RSS + λ pΣj=1 |βj|

This is the L1 penalty, and the lasso is also called L1 regularization.
The L1 norm is another way to measure the length of the coefficients: walk from the origin along the axes only, like city blocks, instead of cutting straight across:

L1 norm = |β1| + |β2| + … + |βp|

Ridge charges for the straight-line (L2) length, the lasso for the city-block (L1) length.

Both penalties shrink. The difference shows in the constrained form.
The lasso keeps the RSS, and limits |β1| + |β2| ≤ s: every pair of coefficients whose L1 length is at most s.
That constraint region is a diamond, with its corners on the axes, where ridge's is a round disk.
The RSS ellipses often reach a diamond first at one of its corners, and at a corner one coefficient is exactly zero.

The same idea in numbers: with β2, a small coefficient costs almost nothing (taking one from 0.1 down to 0 saves only 0.01λ), so ridge always keeps a little of every feature.
With |β|, every unit costs the same λ, however small the coefficient, so a feature whose contribution to the fit is worth less than that price is dropped completely.

Below λ ≈ 280, the ellipse touches the diamond on one of its sides, and the lasso keeps both coefficients.
From about 280 it touches the diamond at its corner on the β1 axis, and stays there as the diamond shrinks: β2 = 0, the right IP0 is out of the model, and the left IP0 carries the effect alone.
At about 670 the diamond shrinks to (0, 0), and the lasso is the null model. With the same λ, ridge's disk still touches the ellipse where both coefficients are non-zero.

This is why the lasso is a selection operator: it selects features automatically, as part of the fit.
Between correlated features it tends to keep one and drop the others, where ridge would share the weight between them.

Key idea

Ridge shrinks every coefficient and keeps every feature. The lasso shrinks too, and sets some coefficients to exactly zero: it selects features as part of the fit, and the larger λ, the fewer features it keeps.

05

Tuning λ

Back to all 360 regions and the 60 training subjects, where least squares could not even be fitted.
Ridge and the lasso can be fitted for any λ > 0, and every λ gives a different model. How should we choose λ?

λ is a setting like the number of regions k two tabs ago, so we choose it the same way: by cross-validation, on the training set only.
We try a grid of values and keep the one with the lowest MSECV. As always, the features are standardized inside every fold, on that fold's training subjects.

On the far left the penalty is negligible, and the 60 training subjects are fitted perfectly.

Cross-validation chooses λ ≈ 141. Its test MSE is 34.2.

Lasso. Now read from right to left. At a large λ every coefficient is exactly zero, and as λ decreases, regions enter the model one at a time.
Cross-validation chooses λ ≈ 50, where 34 of the 360 regions have a coefficient. The lasso picked these 34 by itself, without us ranking anything, and its test MSE is 41.3.

For comparison: keeping the 14 regions most correlated with age gave 44.7, and predicting the mean age for everyone gives 78.6.

Key idea

λ is chosen like any other flexibility setting: by cross-validation, on the training data. A small λ gives a flexible model with high variance, a large λ a rigid one with high bias.

06

Elastic net

The elastic net uses both penalties at once, the L1 penalty of the lasso and the L2 penalty of ridge:

L(β, λ) = RSS + λ1 pΣj=1 |βj| + λ2 pΣj=1 βj2

It is usually written with one strength λ, and a mixing weight α between 0 and 1:

L(β, λ) = RSS + λ (α pΣj=1 |βj| + (1 − α) pΣj=1 βj2)

α = 1 is the lasso, and α = 0 is ridge.
In between, it can still set coefficients to exactly zero like the lasso, but it shares the weight between correlated features like ridge, instead of keeping one and dropping the rest.
There are now two settings, λ and α, and cross-validation searches a grid of pairs.

Watch the names in scikit-learn: our λ is called alpha there, and our α is called l1_ratio, the share of the L1 penalty.
Their values are scaled differently from ours: the note at the end of the next tab gives the exact translation.

Key idea

The elastic net mixes the L1 and L2 penalties, with α setting the mix: α = 1 is the lasso, α = 0 is ridge. It can select features while sharing the weight between correlated ones.

07

Regularized logistic regression

The penalties are not specific to least squares.
Logistic regression (class 4) is fitted by maximizing the log-likelihood. Regularized logistic regression minimizes the negative log-likelihood, plus the same penalty:

L(β, λ) = −log-likelihood(β) + λ pΣj=1 βj2

That is the L2 penalty of ridge. The L1 penalty of the lasso works the same way, and so does their elastic-net mix.
Everything from the previous tabs carries over: scale the features first, and choose the strength by cross-validation.

In scikit-learn, LogisticRegression is regularized by default, with the L2 penalty.
Its strength is set by C, which works the other way around from λ (C = 1/(2λ) in our notation): a small C means a strong penalty.
The default is C = 1, and like λ it should be tuned by cross-validation.

Not only regression

Logistic regression is just one example. Almost every model has a setting that limits how flexible it can be, and choosing that setting is regularization in the broad sense.
We have already met one: in KNN, a larger K averages more neighbours, and gives a smoother, less flexible fit.
Later in the course, decision trees are limited by how deep they may grow, boosting by its learning rate, and neural networks by weight decay, which is the L2 penalty again.
Each of these settings plays the role of λ, and each is chosen the same way: by cross-validation.

Key idea

The same penalties regularize logistic regression, where scikit-learn sets the strength with C: the smaller C, the stronger the penalty. Most models have a setting like λ that limits their flexibility, and it is always chosen by cross-validation.

A note on scikit-learn's settings

scikit-learn names and scales the penalty strength differently in each model. This is how each setting translates to our λ, with n the number of training subjects:

scikit-learnminimizesour λ
Ridge(alpha)RSS + alpha·Σβj2λ = alpha
Lasso(alpha)RSS/(2n) + alpha·Σ|βj|λ = 2n·alpha
ElasticNet(alpha, l1_ratio)RSS/(2n) + alpha·l1_ratio·Σ|βj|
+ alpha·(1 − l1_ratio)/2·Σβj2		λ1 = 2n·alpha·l1_ratio
λ2 = n·alpha·(1 − l1_ratio)
LogisticRegression(C), L2, the defaultC·(−log-likelihood) + Σβj2/2λ = 1/(2C)
LogisticRegression(C), L1C·(−log-likelihood) + Σ|βj|λ = 1/C

Only the names and the scales differ. Each setting plays the role of λ, and is tuned by cross-validation in the same way.
For the elastic net, λ1 and λ2 are the two strengths in the first elastic-net formula of the previous tab.

08

Glossary

Everything introduced today, on top of the glossaries from classes 1 to 4.

p ≫ n
Many more features than subjects. Least squares then fits the training data perfectly, in infinitely many ways.
feature selection
Choosing which features go into the model. A form of fitting, so it is done inside every CV fold.
selection by
a criterion
Scoring every feature on its own, by its correlation with y or a t- or F-statistic, and keeping the best k.
regularization
Adding a penalty on the size of the coefficients to the loss, so the fit keeps them small.
penalty
The added part of the loss: λ times the size of the coefficients. The intercept is not penalized.
λ, regularization parameter
The price of size. λ = 0 is least squares, λ → ∞ the null model. Chosen by cross-validation.
shrinkage
Coefficients pulled toward zero by the penalty.
null model
Every βj = 0: predicting the mean of y for everyone.
ridge regression
RSS + λΣβj2, L2 regularization. Shrinks every coefficient, sets none to exactly zero, and shares the weight between correlated features.
lasso regression
RSS + λΣ|βj|, L1 regularization. Shrinks, and sets some coefficients to exactly zero: it selects features as part of the fit.
L2 penalty
λΣβj2: λ times the squared L2 norm, the straight-line length of the coefficients. Its constraint region is a disk.
L1 penalty
λΣ|βj|: λ times the L1 norm, the city-block length of the coefficients. Its constraint region is a diamond.
constrained form
The second way to write the penalty: least squares, subject to Σβj2 ≤ s (ridge) or Σ|βj| ≤ s (lasso). A larger λ is a smaller s.
constraint region
The coefficients the constraint allows. Ridge's is a disk, the lasso's a diamond with its corners on the axes.
loss surface
The RSS drawn over the coefficients, as contours around least squares.
coefficient path
Every coefficient plotted against λ.
standardization
(x − x)/sd(x), so the penalty treats every feature alike. The mean and SD come from the training data.
elastic net
The L1 and L2 penalties at once, mixed by α: α = 1 is the lasso, α = 0 is ridge.
C
The strength setting of scikit-learn's LogisticRegression, the inverse of λ: a small C is a strong penalty.
